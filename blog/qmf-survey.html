Survey on Quantum Multimodal Fusion

2026.05
Quantum Multimodal Fusion Survey VQC

A literature survey of quantum approaches to multimodal learning and fusion. We review over 15 recent works and categorize them by their fusion strategy.

Background

Multimodal fusion aims to integrate complementary features across heterogeneous data modalities (e.g., text, image, audio, LiDAR) to enhance perception and decision-making. Classical approaches are dominated by feature-level fusion (deep neural networks with high accuracy but poor interpretability) and decision-level fusion (interpretable but lower accuracy).

Quantum machine learning offers a fundamentally different paradigm. By encoding classical data into quantum states (Hilbert space), variational quantum circuits (VQC) can capture non-linear correlations with exponentially fewer parameters. Quantum entanglement provides a natural mechanism for modeling cross-modal dependencies. This has inspired a growing body of work on quantum multimodal fusion.

Quantum Data Encoding

Before fusion, classical features must be encoded into quantum states. Common encoding strategies include:

  • Amplitude Encoding (AE): Embeds $d$ features into the probability amplitudes of $n = \log_2(d)$ qubits. Qubit-efficient but requires deep preparation circuits and creates pre-entangled states.
  • Angle Encoding (Qubit Encoding): Maps each feature to the rotation angle of a qubit via $R_y$ gates. Produces separable product states: $|\psi\rangle = \bigotimes_{j=1}^{d} R_y(x_j)|0\rangle_j$. Well-suited for parallel bit-wise fusion.
  • Dense Qubit Encoding (DQE): A compromise between AE and angle encoding, using $O(n/2)$ qubits with constant depth.
  • Hybrid Direct / Hybrid Angle Encoding: Block-based approaches trading representation capacity for normalization stability.

For multimodal fusion specifically, angle encoding is the dominant choice because it produces separable states, allowing the fusion circuit to be the sole source of cross-modal entanglement.

Taxonomy of Quantum Multimodal Fusion

Based on our survey, quantum multimodal fusion methods can be categorized into three main paradigms:

1. Explicit Quantum Fusion

Methods with a clear, identifiable fusion step after encoding each modality separately. The fusion circuit creates targeted entanglement between modalities, often through controlled gates.

  • CCNOT-based Fusion (Evidential Vertical QFL): Uses Toffoli (CCNOT) gates where two qubits from different modalities control a target qubit. Information is transferred via controlled-NOT operations.
  • CNOT Ring Fusion (mmQFL, QMNN): Applies a ring of CNOT gates across modalities. In mmQFL, each modality is first processed by its own PQC, then a quantum fusion layer (CNOT ring) combines them before measurement. In QMNN, multi-stride CNOT patterns (k=1,3,6) create entanglement between modality pairs.
  • All-to-All Fusion: A fully connected entanglement pattern where every qubit from one modality interacts with every qubit from another. Maximum correlation capture but high circuit depth.
  • Circuit-Block Fusion: A structured, periodic CNOT pattern offering a practical balance between entangling capability and trainability.

2. Implicit Quantum Fusion

Methods that directly concatenate all modalities into a single long feature vector and encode it into quantum states. Fusion occurs implicitly within the VQC during training, with no separate fusion operation.

  • Amplitude Encoding Fusion (Expressive & Scalable Quantum Fusion): All modality features are directly amplitude-encoded into $\log_2(\sum |\text{features}|)$ qubits using index-controlled $R_y$ gates. Fusion is entangled with representation learning, making it hard to interpret.

3. Classical-Layer Fusion

The vast majority of existing quantum multimodal models perform fusion at the classical level. Quantum circuits are used either for unimodal feature extraction or as a post-fusion processing step, but the fusion itself happens through classical operations (concatenation, attention, residual networks).

  • QMFND: Multilayer fully-connected layers fuse classical features before quantum encoding. Reference value limited.
  • QMLSC: Uses residual networks for cross-modal fusion at the classical level, with quantum encoding only at the end.
  • QFNN: Combines fuzzy logic with quantum neural networks; fusion is essentially classical.
  • QViLa: Vision-language features are concatenated (1024-dim), FC-reduced to 4 qubits for a shallow quantum circuit. Quantum layer has minimal effect.
  • QNMF: "Quantum-Classical-Quantum" pipeline — QCNN extracts image features, classical concatenation, then amplitude encoding. Fusion is classical.
  • MQFL-FHE: Quantum layers process each modality separately; measurement results are fused classically.
  • ITQT-MSA: Quantum-enhanced unimodal extraction only; cross-modal interaction uses classical Transformer attention.

These methods, while incorporating quantum components, do not fundamentally advance quantum fusion mechanisms.

Key Challenges and Observations

  • Barren Plateaus: Increasing qubit count leads to gradient vanishing. Most works use very few qubits (4–10), severely limiting expressiveness.
  • Lack of Interpretability: Most quantum fusion circuits remain "black-box" — the physical meaning of entanglement for cross-modal interaction is unclear.
  • Classical Dominance: The majority of works use quantum circuits only peripherally, with fusion handled classically. These cannot be considered true quantum multimodal fusion.
  • Scalability: Current NISQ hardware limits qubit count, restricting these models to very low-dimensional features.
  • Parameter Efficiency: A key claimed advantage of quantum models — exponential Hilbert space with polynomial parameters — is often underutilized.

Summary

The field of quantum multimodal fusion is rapidly growing but still nascent. Most existing works either perform fusion classically or use black-box quantum circuits without interpretability. There is a clear gap for methods that achieve interpretable quantum fusion with parameter efficiency and competitive accuracy. Our QCMM framework (see next post) addresses these challenges by mapping Dempster-Shafer evidence theory to quantum circuits.
